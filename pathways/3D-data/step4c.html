Orthophotos, Renders and Simulations

3D objects can be used to produce 2D views of an object such as profiles, orthophotos, and cross-sections that can be precisely measured. Renders are another static or video output. These are visual representations using computer graphics techniques. Simulations are similar in practice to simple renders but are more complex.

Orthophotos, profiles, and cross sections

If the 3D model produced has been scaled it can be used to produce accurate maps and drawings. Orthophotos are zenithal images, normally of buildings or the earth's surface, that have been corrected for distortions. When the image exported is not zenithal these images are normally called profiles, for example from the side of an object or a building. When the image represents a view that cuts through the 3D model it is called a cross section. All of these images can be used as a base to draw accurate plans and maps.

An alternative to exporting them as images is to export them as DEM, or Digital Elevation Model. This is a digital representation of the topography or relief of a terrain, a building, or an object. Each pixel will contain information about elevation. This can be a local elevation, for example how tall the object is at that point, or when working with geographic data, it will be the elevation above mean sea level.

Renders

A render refers to the process of generating a visual representation, image or video, from a three-dimensional (3D) model or scene using computer graphics techniques. The goal of rendering is to create a reality that simulates how the 3D objects in the scene would appear when viewed from a certain perspective, under specific lighting conditions, and with various materials and textures applied.

Rendering involves several steps and calculations to transform the raw 3D data into a visually appealing and accurate image. Below is an overview of the rendering process:

  • Geometry Processing: The 3D model's geometric information is processed. This step involves transforming the model's coordinates to the desired view and applying transformations like scaling, rotation, and translation.
  • Camera Setup: A virtual camera is positioned within the 3D scene to define the viewpoint from which the scene will be rendered.
  • Lighting: Virtual light sources are placed in the scene to simulate lighting conditions.
  • Materials and Textures: Materials and textures are applied to the 3D objects to simulate their visual appearance. Materials define how light interacts with surfaces, such as shiny, matte, or reflective surfaces, while textures provide surface details like patterns and colours.
  • Shading: Shading algorithms are used to compute the final colour of each pixel on the image plane based on factors like lighting, material properties, and viewing angle.
  • Rasterization: The 3D scene is projected onto a 2D image plane, breaking down the objects into pixels. This process involves determining which parts of the 3D objects are visible and should contribute to the final image.

Rendering can be resource-intensive and time-consuming, especially for complex scenes and video rendering, where each step needs to be replicated for each frame.

Simulations

Simulations are similar in practice to simple renders but are more complex. By accurately representing physical objects and their interactions, 3D models enable researchers and designers to study complex systems, and predict outcomes. Whether simulating the aerodynamics of an aircraft, the sound propagation on an open square, or the natural light interaction with buildings, simulations provide insights that streamline experimentation and allow us to test hypotheses.

These simulations not only enhance our understanding of intricate processes but have also revolutionised the way researchers can reconstruct environments and sites that are no longer preserved. Digitally reconstructing historical sites and artefacts offers immersive experiences that allow researchers and enthusiasts to explore and engage with the past in unprecedented ways.
