Digitised Material as Text

Extracting text from digitised material is valuable for both research methods and outputs. Digital textual research methods are wide-ranging and there are many ways to approach and analyse your text, as well as present your text afterwards. Your digital corpus will need to be constructed with your purpose in mind and it is always best to document your decision making through creating and working with extracted text for replicability. Below are two popular ways of using digital text for research and presentation purposes.

Text Analysis

Once your material is digitised and you have a clean text corpus, see the Text Extraction & Preparation section for more details, you can perform text analysis on the material. Text analysis is a powerful computational tool that can allow you to see patterns across vast amounts of written material to answer particular research questions. Depending on your research questions, text analysis enables you to unpick a vast amount of information from your corpus, including word frequency distributions, contexts words are used in, known as concordances, vocabulary choices, lexical diversity, attitudes or opinions in your text, sentiment analysis, common topics, topic modelling, text similarity and many more.

There are different ways into text analysis. Some are pre-built free tools such as Voyant Tools , AntConc, and LancsBox but if you would like to do something more specific programming opens up more opportunities. If this interests you, consider following the Text Analysis Pathway or the Sentiment Analysis Pathway.

Digital Text Editions

Digital editions are texts published electronically that use electronic tools to enhance the use and understanding of the text. They go beyond the basic presentation of digitised material to offer different avenues of theory, method and practice. Often called digital scholarly editions, these texts take many formats but often combine images of analogue texts alongside transcriptions, translations and the option for interactive page elements. The Electronic Beowulf by Kevin Kiernan is a popular example of a digital scholarly edition.

Textual data can be encoded using Extensible Markup Language (XML) to describe, store and transmit data. This means that texts can be read in both human and machine-readable formats, and information such as lines, paragraphs and chapters are encoded so the structure and content can be replicated. The Text Encoding Initiative (TEI) has developed text standards for consistency across encoded texts and their website directs to numerous guides and tutorials. If you intend to create a digital scholarly edition you will require high quality images and an accurate text transcription.
