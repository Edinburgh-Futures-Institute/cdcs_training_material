EFI Data-led Research Methods

Evaluate and Report

Once you have performed all the analyses needed on your text, it is fundamental to evaluate and report the results you have got.

The final product needs to be understandable and reproducible by other researchers. To make sure this is the case, you can have your results comply with the FAIR Data Principles. In the long run, making your research FAIR will help its circulation and impact.

How to do it

When evaluating and reporting the results of your text analysis, remember to summarise the corpus or text you are working with, including the corpus statistics from your initial analysis. Data visualisations that helped you study your text will probably be helpful to your audience. Consider which graphs you may want to reuse or modify to communicate your findings. If you are working with messy text, meaning it has digitisation errors or potential typos, be sure to communicate any data cleaning steps you undertook and explain the state of the data to your audience. For an example, check out the notes page of the We Are What We Steal project.

If you are adding information to a document or collection of documents, perhaps by annotating your text, refer to the Text Encoding Initiative (TEI) for a standardised approach to this work.

Remember that many text analysis tools were not built by humanities researchers, so your research question may warrant a different analysis approach or different interpretation than other researchers who have used the same text analysis tool or tools you have. For an example, check out Lisa Marie Rhody’s discussion of her approach to working with stop words in a text analysis project.

FAIR Data Principles

FAIR is an acronym that stands for: Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable. It references a protocol published in 2016 and it is now widely accepted as the standard protocol for data interoperability.

  • Findable

    “The first step in reusing data is to find them. Metadata and data should be easy to find for both humans and computers. Machine-readable metadata are essential for automatic discovery of datasets and services.”

  • Accessible

    “Once the user finds the required data, they need to know how they can be accessed, possibly including authentication and authorisation.”

  • Interoperable

    “The data usually need to be integrated with other data. In addition, the data need to interoperate with applications or workflows for analysis, storage, and processing.”

  • Reusable

    “The ultimate goal of FAIR is to optimise the reuse of data. To achieve this, metadata and data should be well described so that they can be replicated and/or combined in different settings.”
