Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics quantitatively describes or summarises aspects of a dataset.

It helps simplify large quantities of data into a more understandable and easily interpretable form. Each descriptive statistic reduces lots of data into a simpler summary.

There are different ways to do so: via graphs, calculations, and tables. The important things to keep in mind are to convey the results clearly, to always state the sample size (how many records are in your datasets), and to understand the level of simplification involved (certain trends will be lost if you generalise the analysis too much).

If you are planning to use graphic visualisations for your descriptive analysis, you should also have a look at the Building Graphs step in the Data Visualisation Pathway.

Distribution

One of the most common forms of descriptive analysis is the description of data distribution.

In theory, a sample collecting numerical information about a variable (for example, the height of a group of people) would have a normal distribution, meaning that if plotted it would resemble a bell-shaped curve.

Going back to the people heights example, this means that many people will have heights clustering around a central value, while progressively fewer people will have much lower or higher heights.

Normally distributed samples are at the basis of many inferential statistics methods, and therefore it is very important to understand your sample before choosing a specific method.

However, this principle is often more applicable to the exact sciences than to the Social Sciences and Humanities. Data in these disciplines are often not normally distributed, and this needs to be taken into consideration during analysis.

This issue can partially be addressed through the Central Limit Theorem.

Measures of Central Tendency and Dispersion

Two additional characteristics that can be examined for each variable are:

  • Measures of Central Tendency — how values cluster together
  • Measures of Dispersion — how values spread out

Measures of Central Tendency

  • Arithmetic Mean: the average value of the sample (sum of all values divided by the number of values)
  • Median: the middle value splitting the higher and lower halves of the sample
  • Mode: the most frequently repeated value in a sample

Measures of Dispersion

  • Variance: the squared mean difference between each value and the mean, divided by the sample size minus one
  • Standard Deviation (SD): a measure of the amount of variation or dispersion within a set of values. A low standard deviation indicates that values tend to be close to the mean.
